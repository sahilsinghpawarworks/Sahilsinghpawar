Happy Faces Foundation

ResearchSystems DesignStrategy Design

(Scroll for more)↓

Made for Happy Faces Foundation, Ahmedabad

Approach is Systems thinking · 4P framework

Focus is Women's livelihoods & social enterprise

(Details)

Mapped the Happy Faces Foundation ecosystem using the 4P framework (People, Places, Process and Practice) to uncover systemic gaps and opportunities, then proposed targeted design interventions to strengthen its processes, practices and social impact.

Methods: Site study, actor mapping, process & material-flow mapping, system map, causal loops, iceberg model

(A closer look)

(01)Context

Happy Faces Foundation (HFF) is a grassroots organisation that helps underprivileged women become self-reliant through skill development and entrepreneurship. Started by social entrepreneur Mrs. Reeta Bhagat to create opportunities rather than offer charity, it trains women to craft handmade products, from eco-friendly goods to traditional crafts, and helps them reach local and global markets.

The foundation reports training over 300 artisans and reaching more than 500 households. During COVID-19, its women turned their sewing machines to affordable, reusable cotton masks, meeting a public need while keeping an income coming in.

(02)The problem

HFF works like most making organisations: raw material comes in, the women transform it, and finished products go out to market. To strengthen it, we first had to see the whole system: who is involved, where the work happens, how products are made and which practices hold it together. The brief was to map HFF's ecosystem through the 4P lens of People, Places, Process and Practice, identify the gaps and propose design interventions.

(03)The process

Studying the centre

We studied the Ahmedabad centre on site: its key actors, from the floor manager and coordinators to the women makers, the ground-floor production unit and the display unit. We followed its three product lines from raw material to finished goods, mapping process, material and information flows for each:

  • Macramé: keychains, bags, torans, wind chimes and décor knotted from cotton cords.
  • Banana fibre: fibre from banana stems turned into handmade paper, tote bags, wallets and cushions.
  • Dry nashta: traditional ready-to-eat snacks such as sev mamra, methi sticks and methi puris.

Seeing the system

A system map connected the inner environment (raw materials, designers, institutes, product development) with the outer one (market, natural environment, information networks, practices). Causal loops then showed the growth levers and pressure points. Reinforcing loops: income-driven motivation, skill-building through training, trust-based word of mouth and design innovation. Balancing loops: fatigue from overproduction, and quality drops as production scales.

(04)The solution

We proposed an intervention at Donella Meadows' leverage point #6, the structure of information flows: an ongoing collaboration between design students and HFF's artisans, so ideas, skills and feedback keep moving through the system.

  • Formalise partnerships with design schools through semester-long or live projects with artisans.
  • Create a joint co-creation platform, a shared space for artisans and students, virtual or physical.
  • Set aside a share of revenue as a "Design Collaboration Fund" so the loop keeps going.
  • Showcase co-created products through exhibitions, social media and artisan narratives.
  • Capture feedback from customers and artisans to keep improving the process.

Alongside this, we proposed positioning HFF clearly as a social enterprise, part NGO and part business, and adopting zero-waste, circular production methods.

(05)The impact

The intervention is designed to change how people connect, not just what they make. Artisans gain new skills and a greater sense of ownership and pride; designs become more distinctive and meaningful, drawing customers who value the story behind each product. As sales grow, more can be invested back into the partnerships, creating a cycle of growth, confidence and shared innovation.

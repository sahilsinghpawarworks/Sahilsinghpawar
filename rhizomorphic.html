Rhizomorphic

ResearchStrategy Design

(Scroll for more)↓

Focus is Bio-material design innovation

Material is Wheat, moong, bajra & chia roots

Team is 5 designers

(Details)

Material-led research into a sustainable rhizomorphic bio-material: a composite made by guiding the growth of plant roots into a dense, fibrous network, then strengthening it with natural coatings. The findings were translated into product concepts that could scale, with a focus on usability, durability and eco-conscious design.

Methods: Trend matrix, innovation evolution map, precedent study, stakeholder interviews, material experiments

(A closer look)

(01)Context

Within agriculture we explored biomaterials, materials that are grown rather than manufactured. In farming and hydroponics alike, attention goes to the upper part of the plant, while roots are discarded as waste despite their structural strength, binding ability and natural growth patterns.

Roots form interwoven, self-organising networks that adapt and regenerate. That inspired Rhizomorphic Design: an approach that grows horizontally and adaptively like roots, has no central hierarchy, and encourages co-growth between nature and design.

(02)The problem

Could an overlooked agricultural by-product become a meaningful, sustainable material? The challenge was to turn random root growth into a material with consistent strength, durability and a finish people would accept, and to find where it could replace something worse.

(03)The process

Seven stages

We worked through seven stages: Sense Intent, Know Context, Know People, Frame Insights, Explore Concepts, Frame Solutions and Realise Offerings.

Context and precedents

A trend matrix across technology, business, culture and policy showed roots being explored as structural and hybrid materials, root-grown materials forming new value chains, and a cultural shift towards materials valued for their origin and growth. Regulation and scale remain the barriers. We studied precedents such as Diana Scherer's root-woven textiles, Baubotanik living buildings, the living root bridges of Meghalaya and Full Grown furniture.

Talking to people

Interviews with farmers, artisans and consumers surfaced four insights: agri-waste is hidden value (farmers see waste where artisans and consumers see potential); the story matters more than the material (half of consumers rated origin stories "very important"); demonstration is needed, because seeing and touching products reduces confusion and fear; and the material must match cultural aesthetics, with consumers preferring earthy, premium, natural-looking products.

(04)The solution

We guided root growth with laser-cut and 3D-printed templates, using the roots' own drive towards water and nutrients to impose a structure, such as a honeycomb for porosity and insulation or a grid for strength. A roughly 14–20 day cycle gives roots that are mature enough to form a woven mat but still flexible.

Testing growing media showed coco coir worked best: agar released roots easily but grew fungus, and cotton held too much water. After growth, the mats were treated with gum arabic and tannin resin, gelatin and bioplastic layers, and cotton and coir blends. The enhanced rhizomorphic sample is rigid and stiffer, keeps high thermal insulation thanks to the roots' porosity, and gains water resistance from the coir.

(05)The offerings

Wheat roots bring porosity, light weight, tensile strength and biodegradability. We mapped these to everyday single-use products the material could replace: food and beverage containers, wraps and films, utensils, sachets and pouches, brewing aids, hygiene items, and fashion and lifestyle accessories.

Beyond the Shelf(2025)

AR ExperiencesResearchUX Design

(Scroll for more)↓

Made for Skyblue Experience Centre

Team is Prachi Sandal, Simran Kaur Somal, Sahil Pawar, Pritam Kumar Tudu

Focus is Multisensory AR in phygital retail

Date is October 2025

(Details)

Designing and piloting a multisensory Augmented Reality (AR) screen system for the Skyblue Experience Centre, to close the gap in phygital interaction. Grounded in research on AR, multisensory experience design and cognitive psychology, the intervention uses visual, auditory and interactive cues to spark attention, curiosity, memory and decision-making, making Skyblue an immersive, behaviourally informed retail space.

Methods: Literature review, thematic analysis, Activity Theory, UTAUT2, case studies, survey (n=100), interviews (n=20), Kano model, personas, empathy & affinity mapping, TAM validation

Tools: TouchDesigner, Python, Azure Face API, Figma

(A closer look)

(01)Context

Skyblue, founded in 2008 by Shailesh Pithadia, is a stationery and creative-supplies brand whose Experience Centre puts a huge range of products on the shelf. Retail is going phygital: shoppers now expect stores to offer the interactivity and personalisation they get online, and immersive tech like AR is how leading brands meet that.

We studied how AR influences thinking and feeling in-store, through a literature review and thematic analysis, Activity Theory (ATF) and UTAUT2, and case studies such as Memory Bytes at IIT Madras, Burberry and Gucci.

(02)The problem

Skyblue’s retail experience offers a wide range of stationery but lacks interactive personalisation, emotional engagement and creative interactivity. Young, creative shoppers especially feel overwhelmed by choice and struggle to find products that match their mood or intention, which leads to a disconnected, uninspired shopping journey.

(03)The process

Survey and interviews

A survey of 100 shoppers and 20 in-depth interviews showed a clear appetite for AR:

  • 73% are open or very open to using AR in-store.
  • 81% agree AR would influence their purchase decisions.
  • 9 in 10 would visit the store just to experience AR.

Visual displays, product variety and interactivity were the top factors shaping the experience. Shoppers wanted to try before buying, felt lost in the layout and the variety, and leaned heavily on busy staff for recommendations.

Making sense of it

A Kano analysis showed users need clarity and guidance first; only once those must-be needs are met do performance and delight features lift satisfaction. Personas, empathy maps and affinity clustering led to four need gaps: overwhelming choice, lack of interactive personalisation, no creative or interactive retail, and emotional disconnection.

(04)The solution

The Aura Scanner: an in-store AR screen that reads the shopper’s facial expression, detects their current emotion and wraps them in a matching colour aura through artistic AR overlays. It then recommends stationery to suit the mood, cutting choice overload and making the visit personal and playful.

  • Calm → blue aura → pastels, smooth pens, soft notebooks.
  • Energetic → orange aura → brush pens, creative kits.
  • Focused → green aura → study planners, structured tools.
  • Overwhelmed → violet/grey aura → calming stationery sets.

The emotion → colour → aura mapping is grounded in colour psychology and cross-cultural colour–emotion research.

(05)The prototype

We built a working prototype in TouchDesigner. A webcam frame is captured every 3 seconds, converted by a Python script and sent to the Azure Face API, which returns emotion scores. The script picks the strongest emotion and switches the AR background to match. The hi-fi UI, “What’s your aura?”, explains the experience and makes privacy explicit: detection happens in real time and no photos, video or personal data are stored.

(06)Validation

We tested the concept with the Technology Acceptance Model (1–5 Likert scale):

  • Perceived usefulness: 3.74
  • Perceived ease of use: 3.80
  • Attitude towards use: 3.87
  • Behavioural intention: 3.39

Usefulness, ease of use and attitude all score between 3.7 and 3.9, strong signs of acceptance: people see it as genuinely helpful, not a gimmick. Behavioural intention is moderate, so people like the concept but may need a nudge to use it every visit.
